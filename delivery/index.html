Technical project manager

I own the delivery. Requirements, estimates, sprint plans and timelines, then the daily coordination of development, QA, UI/UX and infrastructure until it is live and supported. Status and risk go to stakeholders in writing, not only in standups.

Technical lead

I set the architecture and the standards, review the code, and keep the build honest: versioned plugins rather than edits to core, tests that run in CI, and an upgrade path. I do this alongside a team that already has a manager.

Both at once

On smaller platforms I am the plan and the hands. The fintech compliance platform was built that way: sole developer and technical lead, with the client's own team running it day to day afterwards.

The pipeline

Every phase ends in a document, not a feeling

This is the shape of a platform delivery I run. The right-hand column is what exists at the end of each phase, and what you can hold me to.

  1. 01Discovery

    What exists today, who touches it, what the platform has to do, and what would make the project fail. Sessions with the people who will actually use it, not only the people who signed for it.

    You end up withRequirements, a risk list, and the constraints written down
  2. 02Brief and estimate

    A business requirement document, an estimate with its assumptions exposed, and a milestone plan. If something in the brief is going to be expensive for a reason you would not expect, you hear it here.

    You end up withBRD, estimate, milestone plan, acceptance criteria
  3. 03Build in sprints

    Sprint planning, a tracked backlog, and coordination across development, QA, UI/UX and infrastructure. Code review and CI on every change. Nothing goes to production that has not been clicked through on staging.

    You end up withA delivery note per milestone: what changed, what to test, what is still open
  4. 04User acceptance

    Your people test against the acceptance criteria agreed in phase two, on a staging copy with realistic data. Defects are triaged with you, so you decide what blocks go-live and what follows it.

    You end up withA triaged defect list and a signed acceptance record
  5. 05Deployment

    A rehearsed release: a written sequence, a backup taken first, a rollback that has been tested, and a window agreed with whoever is affected. Someone is on the call while it happens.

    You end up withA release runbook and a restore procedure your team can execute
  6. 06Post go-live

    The weeks that decide whether a platform is actually yours. Fixes, admin training, and the documentation that lets your team run it without calling me.

    You end up withOperations runbook, admin guides, change log and a handover call
The team around it

Four functions, one plan

A platform delivery is rarely one discipline. The coordination is the job: making sure the infrastructure is ready when the build is, that QA has something stable to test, and that the interface work is not discovered at the end.

I work in Jira or GitHub Issues, whichever your team already lives in, and I do not ask people to report twice.

Stakeholders

Bad news early, in writing

Most delivery failures are communication failures that were visible weeks before anyone said so.

  • Status that is the same for everyone. One written update per milestone, with what changed, what is at risk and what I need a decision on. The sponsor and the developer read the same thing.
  • Risks carry an owner and a date. A risk with neither is a worry, not a risk. If a dependency on your side is going to slip the date, you hear it while there is still room to act.
  • Scope changes get priced, not absorbed. Quietly absorbing a change is how a project arrives late with nobody able to say why.
  • Escalation has a path. Agreed at the start, so raising something is routine rather than a confrontation.
